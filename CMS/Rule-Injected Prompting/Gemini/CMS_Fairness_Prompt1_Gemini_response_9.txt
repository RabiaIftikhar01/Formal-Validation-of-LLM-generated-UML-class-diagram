As a professional Software Engineer, I have analyzed the provided requirements, user stories, use case diagram, and domain model. I have meticulously applied the given design practices and instructions to produce a clear, robust, and maintainable class diagram. The design prioritizes simplicity, loose coupling, and clear responsibilities to ensure the system is extensible and easy to understand for future development and maintenance.

Here's the rationale behind key design decisions, aligning with the provided practices:

1.  **Class Naming & Conventions (Practices 2, 3, 4, 23, 24):**
    *   All classes are named as singular nouns (e.g., `Student`, `CourseOffering`, `Enrollment`).
    *   PascalCase is used for class names (e.g., `CourseOffering`, `NotificationService`).
    *   camelCase is used for attributes and methods (e.g., `enrollmentDate`, `createCourseOffering`).
    *   Enums are uppercase with underscores (e.g., `WAITLISTED`, `ANNOUNCEMENT`).
    *   This ensures consistency and readability.

2.  **User and Role Modeling (Practices 5, 8, 13):**
    *   The `User` abstract class serves as a base for common attributes (`userId`, `name`, `email`) and behaviors (`getInfo`, `receiveNotification`).
    *   `Student` and `Instructor` inherit from `User`, clearly defining their specific roles and operations (`enrollInCourse`, `createCourseOffering`).
    *   This avoids redundancy and adheres to the "avoid multi-level inheritance" practice by keeping the hierarchy shallow.

3.  **Course Structure Refinement (Practices 1, 5, 8, 13, 20):**
    *   Instead of `Undergraduate` and `Postgraduate` as subclasses of `Course` (as in the domain model), `Course` represents the generic course definition (e.g., "Introduction to Programming").
    *   `CourseOffering` represents a specific instance of a `Course` taught in a particular term/year, and it contains a `type: CourseType` attribute (an enum). This simplifies the hierarchy, making it more flexible for new course types without altering the class structure, and avoids multi-level inheritance for offerings.
    *   `CourseMaterial`, `Announcement`, and `Assignment` are modeled as separate classes aggregated by `CourseOffering`. This keeps `CourseOffering`'s responsibilities manageable and avoids "classes with too many attributes" (Practice 5).

4.  **Enrollment as an Association Class (Practice 20):**
    *   `Enrollment` is modeled as an association class between `Student` and `CourseOffering`. This is ideal because `Enrollment` has attributes (`enrollmentDate`, `status`) that describe the relationship itself, not just one of the participating classes. This clearly implements business rules regarding enrollment details (US-SYS2, US-S4).

5.  **Notification System Design (Practices 5, 8, 27):**
    *   A dedicated `Notification` class holds the data for a notification (`message`, `timestamp`, `type`).
    *   `NotificationService` is a separate, stateless class responsible solely for sending notifications. This promotes loose coupling (Practice 27), as other classes (`CourseOffering`, `Instructor`) can *use* this service without being tightly coupled to its implementation details.
    *   The `User` class includes `receiveNotification()` to demonstrate how users interact with notifications.
    *   `CourseOffering` has a private `triggerNotification` helper method that uses `NotificationService` to send notifications related to course events (e.g., new announcements, assignment deadlines), adhering to US-SYS3 and US-I3.

6.  **Attributes and Operations (Practices 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10):**
    *   Attributes are carefully assigned to appropriate classes, ensuring a manageable set per class.
    *   Most attributes have public getter methods (`getUserId()`, `getTitle()`) as per industry standard (Practice 9).
    *   Operations are assigned to classes based on their responsibilities (e.g., `Instructor` creates/updates, `Student` enrolls/views).
    *   Helper methods (like `CourseOffering.triggerNotification`) are marked as private (Practice 10).

7.  **Relationships and Multiplicity (Practices 11, 12, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 21):**
    *   Relationships are identified with clear multiplicities (e.g., `1` to `0..*`), explaining the business rules (Practice 15).
    *   Many associations are unidirectional (e.g., `Instructor` manages `CourseOffering`), reducing coupling and improving reusability and maintainability (Practice 12, 27).
    *   Relationship names (e.g., `manages`, `offers`, `contains`) are used where necessary to clarify the purpose of the association (Practice 18).
    *   Dependencies (`..>`) are used for `CourseOffering ..> NotificationService` to show that `CourseOffering` relies on `NotificationService` to perform its duties without a strong structural coupling.

8.  **Simplification and Readability (Practices 1, 25, 26):**
    *   The model avoids redundancy and strives for the simplest representation that fulfills the requirements.
    *   Explanatory notes are added to clarify complex interactions or design choices (e.g., instructor authorization, notification delivery).
    *   Hidden relationships (`-[hidden]`) are used to improve the layout and prevent clutter, enhancing readability.

This design aims for high internal quality factors (maintainability, extensibility, understandability) which in turn support external quality factors (correctness, reliability).
